Booking a flight is a long, high-stakes form. This is how I took it from research to a high-fidelity prototype, designing the whole flow on my own as the final case study of my Professional Diploma in UX Design.

<Figure src="/work/skyray/hifi-home.png" alt="SkyRay homepage with a black hero and a white search card" caption="The finished SkyRay homepage. The search card is the only thing the eye is asked to find." />

The brief was a flight booking website for a startup airline. I named it SkyRay.

## The problem

Airline interfaces are often cluttered, split across many page loads, and unforgiving of mistakes. The brief asked for something fast, reliable and transparent, and it contained a real tension: how do you balance business goals like upselling and data collection against a user's need for a simple, low-friction booking?

My approach rested on Hick's Law: fewer, simpler choices at each step mean faster decisions. I set three objectives:

- **Reduce friction.** Cut unnecessary steps between search and payment.
- **Match mental models.** Use familiar travel patterns so nothing needs learning.
- **Prevent errors.** A linear flow where dates, passengers and seats can be edited without losing progress.

## Research

I started with the mental models behind flight booking.

**Survey (10 respondents).** 80% prefer a desktop or laptop for the final booking, mostly because flight forms are complex. Ten people is too few for statistical claims, so I treated it as direction, not proof.

**Interviews (8 participants).** Anxiety peaked at checkout, driven by the fear of entering something wrong with no clear way back.

**Competitive benchmarking.** I compared leading booking sites to see which conventions are worth keeping and which flaws to fix.

**Usability tests of an existing flow.** I recorded people booking on a current airline site and took live notes on every pause and frown, 215 observations in all, one column per participant.

**Affinity diagram.** I sorted about 200 of those observations onto Miro as positive (79), neutral (80) or negative (47) stickies, then clustered them by theme: dates and search, seat preferences, baggage, passenger details, payment, desktop versus mobile, and the way people read. The negatives clustered around the same few moments, which is where the journey map below picks up.

<Figure src="/work/skyray/affinity-diagram.png" alt="Affinity diagram on Miro, with sticky notes grouped into clusters" caption="The affinity diagram on Miro: each cluster is a moment in the booking flow, from dates and search to payment." />

Their words did most of the work:

- *"Is it a British Airways website or a flight booking website?"*
- A fare called "Qsuite" with nowhere to find out what it was.
- A date picker that disappears after the outbound choice, so the return date is a guess.
- A "Nationality, as shown in document" label that raised the question: which document?
- A forced log-in pop-up that appeared in the middle of choosing a car hire.

<Callout title="The real problem was structural">The pain wasn't visual. It was the rigid logic of the flow: you can't change your mind, and nothing tells you what you're buying.</Callout>

## What I learned

**1. Fragmented flows feel like an obstacle course.** Every extra page load broke focus. People wanted one direction, from search to confirmation.

**2. No flexibility punishes people who are still comparing.** Changing a date meant starting again. The flow had to be editable and non-destructive.

**3. Upsells arrive at the wrong moment.** Hotel and car offers dropped into the middle of a complex task felt intrusive. People didn't object to the offers, only to the timing, so I moved them to the end.

**4. Desktop is the trusted place to book.** 80% of participants preferred it, and described mobile as too complicated. I designed website-first.

**5. Price decides everything.** 60% named price as their main motivator, and many left when costs weren't clear at fare selection. Price had to be visible early and stay visible.

**6. Ryanair sets the expectations.** 60% had visited an airline site in the last four weeks, often Ryanair, and cited it as the example of too many choices across repeated page loads. I kept familiar conventions and cut the choice overload.

### The journey map

I mapped nine stages, from choosing airports to paying, and for each wrote what worked, what hurt, and the design response.

<Figure src="/work/skyray/journey-map.png" alt="Customer journey map on Miro" caption="The journey map, condensed to what worked and what hurt at each of the nine stages." />

| Stage | What hurt | What I did |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Airports | Unsure whose site it is | Positioning line in the hero; autocomplete shows city and code |
| Dates | No prices; return date vanishes | Price on every day; range stays visible |
| Search | Hard to compare days; codes confuse | A date strip for neighbouring days; city names in plain words |
| Flights | Unexplained fare names | Plain fare names, like "Adult Basic Fare", in the summary |
| Seats | Premium details inconsistent | One seat guide; the price shown under every seat |
| Bags | Included vs paid unclear | "Included" pill against priced add-ons |
| Details | Vague labels | Plain-language labels with helper text |
| Review | Offers shown too early | One summary with edit links; offers after |
| Payment | Accepted cards vary | Every accepted payment method shown up front |

Three decisions came straight from the research: price stays on screen at every step, extras come after the main task and can be skipped, and any completed step can be edited without losing progress.

## Testing the prototype

I built a medium-fidelity Figma prototype and ran moderated usability sessions with **8 participants**. At this stage I was testing the clarity of the information and the structure of the flow, not the visuals.

## High fidelity

### A calm, signage-like system

The moodboard was industrial and Swiss: airport signs, big numerals, lots of grey. I took that literally.

- **Inter, only Inter.** One typeface, and the logo is the word "SkyRay" set in it.
- **A 12-column grid** on a 1440px frame, 40px margins, 20px gutters and a 4px spacing base.
- **Warm greys, a deep blue and one highlight.** Signal lime (`#C8F13A`) is reserved for the single main action on each screen. There's no orange or red in the brand, so an error colour always means an error.

Colours are two-layer Figma variables: raw primitives, then semantic tokens like `bg/default` and `action/cta`. Components only use the semantic layer, so changing one primitive updates every use.

<Figure src="/work/skyray/design-system.png" alt="SkyRay design system foundations: primitive and semantic colour, typography, spacing and radius" caption="The Foundations sheet in Figma: primitive colour, semantic colour, type scale, spacing and radius, all as variables or styles." />

### Feedback changed the look

My first hi-fi pass sat on warm grey and felt flat. The eye went to the large text under the search card instead of the card. I made three changes:

1. A **black hero** reading "Where to next?", so the search card and lime button become the focal points.
2. A **black band on every booking step** holding the progress tabs: current tab lime, completed white, upcoming outlined.
3. **White pages with raised cards**, a hairline border and a soft shadow, in place of grey on grey.

### Review, pay, confirm

Passenger details and the trip summary share one screen, so people can check what they're paying for while they type. The summary is collapsible, so the page stays short, the total sits in a black bar that's hard to miss, and every accepted payment method is visible before anyone types a card number.

## What I'd do differently

This was a strong foundation in user-centred design, and I'd push it further today.

- **Research at scale.** For a real airline I'd add A/B tests on price display and heatmaps to see exactly where people drop off, plus diary studies to map the whole trip.
- **Collaborate earlier.** Working solo, I missed the challenge of other minds. I'd run design sprints with peers and stakeholders to stress-test edge cases.
- **Dig into why laptops feel safer.** Contextual inquiry on mobile booking could find the trust gaps that keep people off their phones for high-stakes purchases.
- **Test the redesign.** The 8-person test ran on the wireframe prototype, not on the high-fidelity SkyRay design. I won't claim it's better until I've put it in front of people.

Designing for airlines is a balance between business profitability and user sanity. This project is where I learned to hold that balance with clarity, error prevention and content strategy.

*Flights, prices and airline names are fictional.*
